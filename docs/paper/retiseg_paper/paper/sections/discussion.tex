\section{Discussion}

Published performance rankings are not a sufficient basis for choosing a method for diabetic retinopathy lesion segmentation. A published score combines three properties that behave differently: whether the number can be reproduced, how the model performs when training and evaluation are fixed, and whether its performance carries over to data from other sources. Each property is shaped by factors outside the architecture: the evaluation implementation, the annotation conventions of the data and the imaging conditions. Separating the three properties is what makes a comparison informative.

\paragraph{Numerical reproducibility.} A reported score describes one checkpoint under one evaluation. The evaluation is often unstated, and the most widely reused implementation adds a model-dependent amount to the AUPR, enough to reorder models. Baselines are mostly carried from earlier papers, so a comparison table places a new method beside numbers produced under other settings. Released training code did not reproduce the released weights. A reported number therefore cannot be read as a property of the method; it becomes comparable only when the implementation is stated and the baselines are rerun under the same evaluation.

\paragraph{Performance under controlled conditions.} With data, training amount and evaluation fixed, the differences among the leading models were of the size of their variation between seeds, and most models could not be separated from the first. The published ranking did not predict this order. Adapted foundation models and a general high-resolution network were at the top, in line with reassessments in three-dimensional medical segmentation\cite{isensee2024revisited}, and lesion-specific decoders did not improve on a general head with the same backbone. The large departures came from identifiable recipe components, such as a coarse output stride or a per-image Dice term, rather than from a family of methods. For foundation models, the mode of use decided the result. Without training they did not find lesions, although they delineated a lesion well once it was located. Once trained on the task they were competitive. Their advantage appears to come from the encoder and the scale of its pretraining, to which retinal pretraining added nothing in our comparison.

\paragraph{Generalization across data sources.} Performance followed the data more than the model. Models kept their performance on sources labelled in the same fine-grained style and lost a third to a half of it on coarse, region-level or ultra-widefield data. Internal rank predicted independent rank well on the first kind of source, less well on coarse and region-labelled data, and not at all on ultra-widefield images. A source labelled like the test data was worth more than a larger source labelled differently, as Playout and Cheriet observed for U-Net-type baselines\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization}, and adding coarse labels to fine ones did not help. Small lesions, which carry the earliest signs of disease, were the most sensitive to input resolution. These effects were as large as most differences between architectures in the main table. An internal test thus describes performance on images labelled and acquired like the training set, a conclusion shared with assessments in other fields\cite{lu2026tcrepitopenaturemethods}.

\paragraph{Implications.} Method papers should report the three properties separately. For reproducibility, they should state the split and the AUPR implementation, compute the exact AUPR inside the field of view at the original resolution, rerun every baseline, and release probability maps so that any metric can be recomputed. For controlled comparisons, they should include a self-configuring baseline and report several seeds and intervals over test images. For generalization, they should report at least one test set from a source not used for training, without averaging across annotation conventions. Users choosing a model should weigh the training data as much as the architecture: fine-grained labels from an imaging setting like their own, and the recall of small lesions on an independent source, are more informative than a mean AUPR on IDRiD. Where computation allows, an ensemble of several retrained models is the strongest option in our results, and the reference model offers a smaller alternative.

\paragraph{Limitations and outlook.} Most comparisons rest on two training sources. Nine models of the main table have three seeds, and the factor experiments use one to five representative models with one seed, so we interpret only differences larger than the seed variation of the main table. IDRiD has 27 test images, and the ultra-widefield set comes from a single centre and device. RETFound was pretrained on the EyePACS images from which Retinal-Lesions and part of FGADR are drawn. Pixel-level labels are graders' judgements, and the clinical consequences of segmentation errors, such as whether a missed or spurious lesion changes the grade or the referral, are not assessed here. We are reviewing model errors with ophthalmologists to address them; in screening, the value of an automated system also depends on image quality, workflow and how graders use its output\cite{beede2020chi,ruamviboonsuk2022thailand,sayres2019integratedgradients}. Future rounds will add multi-seed factor experiments, a ranked row for natural-image encoders under the same head, the tier-2 models and further test sets (e-ophtha\cite{decenciere2013eophthateleophta}, ROC\cite{niemeijer2010roc} and ultra-widefield OCTA).
