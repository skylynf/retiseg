\subsection{Data collection and study design}
We designed a workflow that joins data collection, model collection and four kinds of evaluation (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}a). Eight test sets were prepared from seven datasets, \rn{n.images} images in total (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}b and Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). IDRiD and DDR are the training sources of the main tables, because both have official splits and almost every published number refers to one of them. TJDR, split into its standard-field and 133$^\circ$ ultra-widefield parts, MAPLES-DR, FGADR, Retinal-Lesions and DiaRetDB1 are independent sources: no model in the main tables was trained on them, and every checkpoint is applied to their test sets as it is. The sources differ in how lesions were drawn. Following the analysis of lesion count and lesion area by Playout and Cheriet\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization} we assigned each source to one of four granularity tiers, fine, mixed, coarse and region consensus, and we average results only within a tier.

A fixed search of three bibliographic databases identified 1,389 records; 764 remained after a Boolean gate and de-duplication, 112 entered the full-text queue, and screening of the priority list added 24 methods and 2 datasets to a snowballed corpus, for a ledger of 86 records (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}d). From it we registered \rn{n.models} model variants in five families and recorded for each a reproducibility level from R0 (no code) to R4 (training code, weights, splits and environment) (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}c and Table~\ref{tab:models}). \rn{n.tier1} variants form tier~1 and enter the main comparisons: general segmentation networks (U-Net\cite{ronneberger2015unet}, DeepLabv3\cite{chen2017deeplabv3,chen2018deeplabv3plus}, HRNet\cite{wang2020hrnet}, Swin-Unet\cite{cao2022swinunet}, FCT\cite{tragakis2023fct}, H2Former\cite{he2023h2former}), the self-configuring nnU-Net\cite{isensee2021nnunet}, the fundus-specific M2MRF\cite{liu2023m2mrf} and HACDR-Net\cite{xu2024hacdrnet}, and eight foundation models (SAM\cite{kirillov2023sam}, SAM~2\cite{ravi2024sam2}, SAM~3\cite{carion2025sam3}, MedSAM\cite{ma2024medsam}, SAMed\cite{zhang2023samed}, SAM-Adapter\cite{chen2023samadapter}, SAM2-UNet\cite{xiong2024sam2unet} and RETFound\cite{zhou2023retfound}). \rn{n.tier2} variants in tier~2 are added as time allows and \rn{n.tier3} in tier~3 are audited from their papers only.

Each model can appear under four evaluations that are never mixed in one column. The paper audit records the numbers as published and where they came from. Author weights are released checkpoints scored on official and independent test sets. Author recipes are the authors' pipelines rerun from scratch. Unified retraining applies one protocol, called \Bstd{} below, to the tier-1 models with complete training code. Two test scenarios are reported throughout, as in the template study\cite{lu2026tcrepitopenaturemethods}: the internal test, which is the official test set of the training source, and the independent test. The primary metric is the exact AUPR of each lesion class over all field-of-view pixels of a test set, averaged over the four classes (\mAUPR); Dice, intersection over union and lesion-level recall by lesion size are secondary. Finally, six factors are varied one at a time on one to five representative models (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}e).

\subsection{The numbers in the literature}
We first asked what stands behind the numbers that papers report. Of the \rn{funnel.dr} fundus-specific methods in the registry (17 dedicated architectures and 3 adaptations of foundation models to this task), \rn{funnel.code} have any author code, \rn{funnel.train} have training code and \rn{funnel.full} (M2MRF) comes with weights, splits and an environment description (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}a). Authors of methods without code or weights were contacted with a fixed template; methods that could not be run stay in the denominator.

For every method paper in the ledger (\rn{lit.n.methods} method papers and one prior cross-dataset benchmark\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization}) we extracted the experimental setup and the comparison tables, \rn{lit.base.n} baseline AUPR values on IDRiD and DDR in all (Supplementary Table~3). The official split is the rule: \rn{lit.idrid.official} of the \rn{lit.idrid.n} papers that use IDRiD and \rn{lit.ddr.official} of the \rn{lit.ddr.n} that use DDR state it, and the others give no split or merge the official test images into training. What the papers leave out is how the metric was computed. Of \rn{lit.impl.n} papers whose main metric is AUPR, \rn{lit.impl.notstated} give no implementation, \rn{lit.impl.fixed} integrate over a fixed grid of 11 or 33 thresholds, which is the IDRiD challenge protocol carried on by the evaluation code released with L-Seg and M2MRF, \rn{lit.impl.exact} use the exact average precision and \rn{lit.impl.perimage} averages over images; none states that the evaluation is restricted to the field of view, and \rn{lit.pooled.stated} say whether pixels are pooled over the test set or scores are averaged over images (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}b). Baseline numbers travel between papers. Of the baseline values, \rn{lit.base.rerun}\% were produced by the paper that reports them, \rn{lit.base.copied1}\% are identical to the value in the method's own paper, \rn{lit.base.copied2}\% are identical to a value that an earlier paper had already copied or produced, and \rn{lit.base.unverified}\% cite a source outside the ledger or none. Only \rn{lit.rerun.all} papers state that every baseline was rerun, and \rn{lit.rerun.notstated} say nothing about where their comparison numbers come from. As a consequence one method carries a range of values: L-Seg is reported on IDRiD by \rn{lit.lseg.n} papers with \mAUPR{} from \rn{lit.lseg.lo} to \rn{lit.lseg.hi}, DeepLabv3+ by \rn{lit.deeplab.n} papers from \rn{lit.deeplab.lo} to \rn{lit.deeplab.hi} and U-Net by \rn{lit.unet.n} papers from \rn{lit.unet.lo} to \rn{lit.unet.hi}. Copied values agree to the last digit; the spread comes from the papers that reran a method under their own settings (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}c). The audit also surfaced copied tables whose lesion columns are transposed with respect to the source, a baseline table reproduced verbatim from an earlier paper of the same group without attribution, and a Dice value carried into an AUPR column (Supplementary Table~3).

The metric implementation alone moves the numbers. The evaluation code released with M2MRF, which later work reuses, computes AUPR from 11 thresholds; at threshold 0 every pixel with a non-zero probability is counted as positive, so the recall is 1 and the precision equals the prevalence, which lifts the area under the curve. On the released IDRiD checkpoint this function returns \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.fn}; after the probabilities are stored as 16-bit maps the same function returns \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.stored}, and the exact pooled AUPR inside the field of view is \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.exact}. On DDR the three values are \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.fn}, \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.stored} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.exact} (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}d).
Applied to the probability maps of the retrained models, the 11-threshold implementation changed \mAUPR{} on IDRiD by less than 0.01 for every converged model, but on DDR, where the low-probability tail of the maps carries more weight, it added 0.035--0.039 for nnU-Net, M2MRF and HACDR-Net, 0.015--0.017 for HRNet and Swin-Unet, 0.005 for DeepLabv3 and 0.168 for FCT: the inflation is model-dependent and is largest for the models whose maps are least calibrated, so it reorders models that differ by less than it (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}d,e).

\end{simtext}

\subsection{Performance of the original models}
Released checkpoints were scored with three numbers each: the value printed in the paper, the authors' evaluation function, and our protocol (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}a and Table~\ref{tab:original}). For M2MRF the authors' function reproduced the repository values to the last printed digit on both datasets (\rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.fn} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.fn}), which shows that moving the code to a current framework did not change its output; the paper reports the mean of three runs (\rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.paper} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.paper}). Under our protocol the same checkpoints reach \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.exact} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.exact}, a gap of \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.gap} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.gap} that is almost entirely due to the threshold-0 term of the 11-threshold curve; restricting the evaluation to the field of view and keeping overlapping labels each change the third decimal only (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}b).

Rerunning the authors' recipe is the direct test of whether released code reproduces a reported number. A first rerun of M2MRF on IDRiD, with an effective batch of 1 instead of the authors' 4, reached \rn{e1r.first.fn} with the authors' metric and \rn{e1r.first.exact} with ours. With the authors' batch size of 4 and their 40,000 iterations, the run reached 0.612 with the authors' metric and 0.549 under our protocol, against 0.676 and 0.639 for the released checkpoint and 0.672 for the three-run mean printed in the paper: the released code trains a model that is 6 points below the released weights, a gap several times larger than the seed-to-seed variation of the same architecture under the unified protocol below (s.d.\ 0.010).
\begin{simtext}
Two further seeds on IDRiD and one run on DDR for M2MRF, and one run per source for HACDR-Net, for which no weights are released, complete this column (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}c).
\end{simtext}
The released HRDecoder checkpoints reached 0.707 on IDRiD and 0.461 on DDR under our protocol, as high as any model retrained below; they were trained by their authors on a canvas twice as large as the unified one and on three to five times as many pixels, so the two numbers are not comparable with the main table and are the reason for the backbone-controlled comparison in the next section. Rerun once with the authors' recipe (their geometry, augmentation, schedule and all 54 training images; one process in place of four), HRDecoder reached \rn{e1r.HRDecoder.IDRiD.fn} with the authors' metric and \rn{e1r.HRDecoder.IDRiD.exact} under our protocol on IDRiD, 0.04 and 0.03 below the released checkpoint (0.713 and 0.707). The authors' runner evaluates every 2,000 iterations on the test split and keeps the best checkpoint; the released file is the last iteration, and in our rerun that selection would have added 0.002, so it does not explain the distance between the two. Applied to the independent test sets as released, the checkpoints behaved like the retrained models (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}d): the DDR checkpoint scored higher on IDRiD (0.590) and on the standard-field TJDR set (0.554) than on its own test set (0.461), kept 0.382 on MAPLES-DR, and fell to 0.20--0.28 on the coarsely or regionally labelled sets and to 0.218 on the ultra-widefield images; the IDRiD checkpoint fell on every other source (0.165--0.478).

\subsection{A unified protocol to retrain the models}
Source recipes cannot serve as a common ground for retraining. The recipes of the general-purpose networks were written for natural images or abdominal organs; moved to IDRiD unchanged they differ about 67-fold in the number of training images seen, and those that resize the whole image to a small fixed input discard a large part of the small lesions (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}b). We therefore fixed everything that is not part of a method: data and splits, a canvas of 1,440 pixels across the field of view, the training amount counted in pixels, augmentation, selection of the checkpoint by validation \mAUPR, inference geometry and evaluation. Each model keeps its loss, optimizer, learning rate, weight decay, schedule shape and effective batch (Methods and Table~\ref{tab:recipes}). The training amount was frozen from the validation curves of two models; no test metric was used. We retrained \rn{n.main} models on IDRiD and on DDR, with three seeds for the seven networks that have a source recipe and for the self-configuring baseline, and one seed for the adapted foundation models (two of them, which also serve as representative models below, with three seeds where noted); the rank tests use seed 0 of every model. The source recipes, for the models for which they completed, are reported in Supplementary Table~4.

\subsection{Performance of retrained models on internal tests}
On the internal tests (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}a and Table~\ref{tab:main}), among the networks trained from their published initialisations the general-purpose HRNet-W48 reached the highest \mAUPR{} on both sources, 0.700 $\pm$ 0.004 on IDRiD and 0.447 $\pm$ 0.009 on DDR (mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over three seeds), followed on IDRiD by Swin-Unet (0.689 $\pm$ 0.003) and the self-configuring nnU-Net (0.671, one run), and on DDR by nnU-Net (0.431, mean of three runs spanning 0.427--0.434) and Swin-Unet (0.431 $\pm$ 0.002). The two fundus-specific networks retrained first did not lead under equal conditions: M2MRF reached 0.657 $\pm$ 0.010 and 0.369 $\pm$ 0.011, within 0.02 of the released checkpoint on IDRiD (0.639) and below it on DDR (0.401), and HACDR-Net 0.538 $\pm$ 0.031 and 0.252 $\pm$ 0.017 against 0.688 and 0.504 in its paper. The residual-encoder variant of nnU-Net did not improve on the default plan (0.631 and 0.428). The difference between the first two models, 0.011 on IDRiD and 0.015 on DDR, is of the order of the median seed standard deviation (0.006 and 0.009), whereas the span of the table is ten times larger. IDRiD has 27 test images, and its bootstrap intervals are correspondingly wide; we use it to connect to the literature and base comparisons between architectures on DDR and on the independent tests.

Lesion classes differed more than models. The median AUPR over models was 0.876 for hard exudates and 0.449 for microaneurysms on IDRiD, and 0.598 and 0.148 on DDR; the best microaneurysm score on DDR was 0.174. Two architectures lost this class almost entirely: DeepLabv3, whose torchvision implementation decodes at an output stride of 8 with bilinear upsampling (microaneurysm AUPR 0.101 on IDRiD and 0.043 on DDR, hemorrhages and exudates normal), and FCT, whose recipe trains from scratch with a constant learning rate and did not converge within the budget (0.298 $\pm$ 0.084 on IDRiD, 0.176 on DDR; its three IDRiD seeds span 0.24--0.40). One failure was specific to a loss rather than to an architecture: the reference U-Net, trained on patches with a per-image Dice term smoothed by 1, drove its rare-class channels to zero on DDR (\mAUPR{} 0.119, 0.119 and 0.103 over three seeds, with soft exudate AUPR 0.000), because 83\% of DDR training patches contain no soft exudate and an empty patch rewards an all-zero output. Computing the Dice term over the batch, the first of the two recipe revisions made in this study (Methods), raised U-Net to 0.644 $\pm$ 0.015 on IDRiD and 0.371 $\pm$ 0.009 on DDR; this revision was decided from training and validation curves only and is disclosed as such.
Two adapted foundation models went above it on seed 0, and neither stayed clearly above it over three seeds. SAMed, a low-rank adaptation of SAM ViT-B trained on 512-pixel windows of the 1,440 canvas, reached 0.717 on IDRiD and 0.469 on DDR on seed 0 and 0.706 $\pm$ 0.015 and 0.460 $\pm$ 0.027 over three seeds (its DDR seeds span 0.430--0.482); RETFound with a UPerNet head 0.708 and 0.466 on seed 0 and 0.699 $\pm$ 0.009 and 0.450 $\pm$ 0.018 over three. Their seed-0 margins over HRNet-W48 on DDR (0.022 and 0.019) shrank to 0.013 and 0.003 over three seeds, within the seed scatter of the models themselves, and on IDRiD the three-seed margins (0.006 and $-$0.001) are within the width of the 27-image test set. SAM-Adapter followed (0.706 and 0.433). MedSAM, fully fine-tuned, lost both rare classes on DDR under its registered per-image Dice (microaneurysm AUPR 0.000, soft exudate 0.001; 0.265 overall), the third case of the mechanism described above; with the Dice term pooled over the batch under the rule registered for that case it reached 0.642 on IDRiD and 0.413 on DDR (microaneurysm 0.136, soft exudate 0.361), below the other adapted foundation models on both sources (Methods; the collapsed runs are kept as the registered recipe). On DDR, RETFound and SAMed gave the highest microaneurysm (0.189, 0.173) and soft-exudate (0.546, 0.460) scores of the table. SAM2-UNet, as registered, trained normally on IDRiD (0.636) but lost both rare classes on DDR (microaneurysm AUPR 0.023, soft exudate 0.022; 0.210 overall): its weighted IoU term is computed per image with smoothing 1, the mechanism that emptied the U-Net's rare-class channels, and under the rule registered for that case the term was pooled over the batch and the model retrained on both sources, to 0.664 and 0.360 (Methods; the collapsed run is kept as the registered recipe in the supplementary tables). Of the fundus-specific networks added from their code or papers, TC-Net (0.653 and 0.361), PMCNet (0.652 and 0.312) and WSRFNet (0.582 and 0.234) sat below every general network except FCT, whereas CLC-Net, re-implemented from its paper, reached 0.661 and 0.414, the highest DDR score of the fundus-specific family and above DeepLabv3 and U-Net.
HRDecoder, M2MRF and HRNet-W48 share the HRNet-W48 backbone, so their three rows compare a general head with two lesion-specific decoders on one backbone. Under the unified protocol the general head led on both sources (0.700 and 0.447), M2MRF followed (0.657 and 0.369) and HRDecoder was last (0.647 and 0.296), a span of 0.05 on IDRiD and 0.15 on DDR; the released HRDecoder checkpoints, trained by their authors on a canvas twice as large and on three to five times as many pixels, score 0.707 and 0.461 under the same evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:original}). Within the budget and canvas of the unified protocol the lesion-specific decoders did not add to the backbone.
Three further runs separate the explanations. The authors' recipe, rerun once, gave 0.674 on IDRiD under our protocol (previous section), 0.03 above the unified run and 0.03 below the released checkpoint. Doubling the training amount of the unified protocol at the 1,440-pixel canvas did not close the gap: 0.615 on IDRiD, below the single-budget run (0.647), and 0.304 on DDR against 0.296 (one seed each), whereas the same doubling raised the shared-backbone control HRNet-W48 on DDR from 0.447 to 0.469. The unified protocol at the authors' 2,880-pixel canvas and 1,920-pixel window, with the training amount counted in equivalent epochs at that canvas (the same 21,000 steps, four times the pixels per step), gave 0.627, below the 1,440-pixel run (0.647) although its validation score was higher (0.596 against 0.562 on the ten validation images); hard exudates gained (0.864 against 0.823) and soft exudates lost (0.615 against 0.728). Neither the canvas nor the training amount therefore accounts for the released checkpoint. What the authors' recipe adds beyond them, 0.03--0.05 on one seed, lies in what the protocol replaces: rotations of $\pm45^\circ$ without photometric distortion, training on all 54 images with no validation split, and the last-step checkpoint (Supplementary Table~4).

\subsection{Performance of retrained models on independent tests}
Moving to a source that was never used for training did not by itself cost performance; the annotation tier and the imaging field did (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}b,c and Table~\ref{tab:independent}; seed 0 of the nine supervised models on all eight test sets). The nine models trained on DDR averaged 0.368 \mAUPR{} on the DDR test set and scored higher on the two other standard-field sources with pixel-level labels, 0.525 on IDRiD and 0.485 on the standard-field TJDR set (every model higher; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $P$ = 0.004 on each), and the same on MAPLES-DR (0.361; four of nine lower, $P$ = 0.50). They lost 0.12--0.21 (33--57\%) wherever the labels or the geometry differ from the training source: 0.202 on FGADR and 0.191 on Retinal-Lesions, whose lesions are drawn as coarse regions, 0.247 pixel-level \mAUPR{} against the region-level consensus of DiaRetDB1, and 0.159 on the 133$^\circ$ ultra-widefield TJDR images (every model lower, $P$ = 0.004 on each). The models trained on the 44 IDRiD images started higher internally (mean 0.595) and ended lower on every other source, fine-grained or not: 0.251 on DDR, 0.393 on the standard-field TJDR set and 0.319 on MAPLES-DR (losses of 34--58\%), and 0.154--0.229 on the coarse, region-level and ultra-widefield sets (62--74\%). The 0.23 advantage of IDRiD over DDR on the internal tests therefore reversed outside the training source: on the six independent sets the DDR-trained checkpoint of a model was better than its IDRiD-trained one in 43 of 54 pairs, and in every pair on MAPLES-DR, FGADR and Retinal-Lesions; only on the ultra-widefield images were the IDRiD-trained checkpoints usually ahead (six of nine), consistent with the larger field and higher resolution of their training images.

Internal rank predicted independent rank where the labels were drawn the same way. For DDR-trained models the Spearman correlation between internal \mAUPR{} and the score on IDRiD, the standard-field TJDR set and MAPLES-DR was 0.98, 0.95 and 0.82; HRNet-W48 was first on the internal test, on IDRiD, on the standard-field TJDR set and on the mean of the three fine-grained independent sets (0.407), and the residual-encoder nnU-Net, fourth internally, was first on MAPLES-DR (0.466). On FGADR the correlation was 0.88 (HRNet-W48 first, 0.269); on DiaRetDB1, Retinal-Lesions and the ultra-widefield set it was 0.62, 0.62 and 0.25, and the first place went to models that were third to eighth internally: nnU-Net on DiaRetDB1 (0.325), DeepLabv3, whose output stride of 8 blurs its maps, on Retinal-Lesions (0.271), and HACDR-Net on the ultra-widefield images (0.203). For IDRiD-trained models the correlation was 0.92 on DDR and on MAPLES-DR and 0.40--0.58 elsewhere; DeepLabv3, eighth internally, led on the ultra-widefield, Retinal-Lesions and DiaRetDB1 sets (0.277, 0.261 and 0.322) and HACDR-Net, seventh, on the standard-field TJDR set (0.506). The adapted foundation models followed the same pattern (seed 0). SAMed led the mean of the three fine-grained independent sets from both sources (0.404 from IDRiD, 0.426 from DDR; HRNet-W48 0.375 and 0.407), was first on the standard-field TJDR set from IDRiD (0.559) and on FGADR and Retinal-Lesions from DDR (0.290, 0.278), and lost on the ultra-widefield images like the rest (0.242 and 0.208). RETFound with UPerNet matched it on MAPLES-DR (0.413 and 0.460) and, after the non-converged FCT, was the weakest model on the ultra-widefield images from DDR (0.111): its 224-pixel windows see 1/19 of the 4,256-pixel canvas at a time. The three fundus-specific networks added (TC-Net, PMCNet, WSRFNet) led no column; TC-Net trained on IDRiD matched the best models on MAPLES-DR (0.412).
With the adapted foundation models and the further fundus-specific networks scored, the paired tests across the \rn{n.ranked} models with a seed-0 run on both sources (MedSAM with its registered recipe, whose DDR run lost two classes; the revised run is pending, Methods) read as follows. For models trained on DDR the standard-field TJDR set was higher than the internal test for every model (mean +\rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-std.gain} \mAUPR{}; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank $P$ \rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-std.p}), MAPLES-DR was level with it (mean \rn{indep.DDR.MAPLES-DR.gain}; \rn{indep.DDR.MAPLES-DR.nlower} of \rn{n.ranked} lower, $P$ \rn{indep.DDR.MAPLES-DR.p}), and the three sets whose labels or geometry differ from DDR were lower for every model: by \rn{indep.DDR.FGADR.drop} on FGADR, \rn{indep.DDR.Retinal-Lesions.drop} on Retinal-Lesions and \rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-uwf.drop} on the ultra-widefield set ($P$ \rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-uwf.p} each). For models trained on the 44 IDRiD images every independent set was lower for every model, by \rn{indep.IDRiD.TJDR-std.drop} on the standard-field TJDR set, \rn{indep.IDRiD.MAPLES-DR.drop} on MAPLES-DR, \rn{indep.IDRiD.TJDR-uwf.drop} on the ultra-widefield set, \rn{indep.IDRiD.FGADR.drop} on FGADR and \rn{indep.IDRiD.Retinal-Lesions.drop} on Retinal-Lesions. The order of the sources was the same for both: none or a gain on fine labels at a standard field of view, a loss on mixed and coarse labels, and the largest loss where the imaging geometry changes.

Internal and independent performance were correlated across models (Spearman $\rho$ = \rn{indep.DDR.rho} for DDR-trained and \rn{indep.IDRiD.rho} for IDRiD-trained models, internal \mAUPR{} against the mean of the three fine-grained independent sets), and the model that led the fine-grained independent sets, \rn{indep.DDR.top.model} (\rn{indep.DDR.top.value} from DDR, \rn{indep.IDRiD.top.value} from IDRiD), was \rn{indep.DDR.sametop} as on the internal test. Averaged by family over both sources, the adapted foundation models were ahead on internal (\rn{family.foundation.internal}) and on fine-grained independent tests (\rn{family.foundation.independent}), the self-configuring baseline followed (\rn{family.auto.internal} and \rn{family.auto.independent}), and the fundus-specific (\rn{family.fundus.internal} and \rn{family.fundus.independent}) and general networks (\rn{family.general.internal} and \rn{family.general.independent}) were level with each other (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}d).

Over the eight test-set blocks the Friedman test rejected equal ranks ($\chi^2$ = \rn{cd.chi2}, $P$ \rn{cd.p}, \rn{n.ranked} models). \rn{cd.first.model} had the best average rank (\rn{cd.first.rank}), followed by \rn{cd.second.model} (\rn{cd.second.rank}) and \rn{cd.third.model} (\rn{cd.third.rank}), and \rn{cd.last.model} the worst (\rn{cd.last.rank}); with eight blocks the Nemenyi critical difference is \rn{cd.value} ranks, so \rn{cd.ntop} of the \rn{n.ranked} models were not separable from the first (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}e). The ranking published for the \rn{tau.published.n} models that report an IDRiD \mAUPR{} did not agree with the ranking after retraining (Kendall $\tau$ = \rn{tau.published}, $P$ = \rn{tau.published.p}; Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}f). A threefold change of the learning rate, tested on DDR for one general and one fundus-specific network and judged on validation \mAUPR{} only, changed the order of HRNet-W48 and M2MRF at one third of the learning rate (0.524 against 0.553) and not at three times it (0.630 against 0.525; main-table rates 0.617 and 0.558), so the main-table learning rates were kept (Extended Data Fig.~7).
Averaging the probability maps of the retrained models, in compositions fixed before any ensemble was scored (Methods), gave the highest scores of the study on every test set (Table~\ref{tab:independent}, lower block; seed-0 members, FGADR and Retinal-Lesions pending). From DDR, the mean of all 18 members (the models with a seed-0 run when the compositions were fixed; MedSAM is not a member) reached 0.505 on the DDR test set and the mean of the five best by validation \mAUPR{} 0.511, against 0.469 for the best member; the same two ensembles reached 0.679 and 0.680 on IDRiD (best member 0.645), 0.639 and 0.652 on the standard-field TJDR set (0.624), 0.497 and 0.506 on MAPLES-DR (0.466) and 0.269 and 0.242 on the ultra-widefield images (0.208; the retrained MedSAM, which is not a member, reached 0.243 there). From IDRiD the mean of all members reached 0.764 on its own test set (best member 0.717), 0.594 on the standard-field TJDR set (0.559) and 0.400 on DDR (0.380). The gain over the best member was 0.02--0.06 \mAUPR{} on every set, and the four adapted foundation models averaged by themselves matched the ensemble of all models (0.509 on DDR, 0.676 on IDRiD, 0.657 on the standard-field TJDR set), whereas the seven fundus-specific networks averaged by themselves gave the weakest family ensemble (0.444 on DDR). Ensembles are a reference for users and do not enter the rank tests above.

DiaRetDB1 gives the comparison a human reference. Each of its four experts, scored against the consensus of the other three with the same protocol, reached a pixel-level \mAUPR{} of 0.15--0.28 (median 0.21) and an image-level AUC of 0.87--0.93 (Table~\ref{tab:independent}, bottom block; a four-level confidence map yields a four-point precision--recall curve, so the pixel-level value is a lower bound). The retrained models scored against the four-expert consensus spanned 0.09--0.35 pixel-level \mAUPR{} (median 0.28 from DDR, 0.24 from IDRiD) and 0.71--0.94 image-level AUC (median 0.83), so on pixel-level agreement with the consensus all but one or two of the models lie within or above the band of the experts, whereas on image-level detection only three (IDRiD-trained) and five (DDR-trained) of \rn{n.ranked} reach the experts' 0.87; the experts' own microaneurysm agreement was the lowest of the four classes (AUPR 0.001--0.022 against 0.039 for the median model), which says as much about the consensus rule as about the models.

\subsection{Foundation models under four kinds of use}
Results for segmentation foundation models are often quoted without saying how the model was used. We separated four kinds of use and report them side by side without ranking one against another (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}a and Table~\ref{tab:foundation}): zero-shot use without any label; prompts derived from the ground truth, which is an upper bound and not a usable setting; parameter-efficient fine-tuning; and an encoder with a trained decoder or full fine-tuning.

\begin{simtext}
Zero-shot use did not segment DR lesions. The any-lesion AUPR of the class-agnostic maps of SAM and SAM~2 and of SAM~3's text prompts stayed far below the trained models (best: \rn{fm.zero.model}, \rn{fm.zero.value} on IDRiD, against \rn{fm.any.ref.nnunet.IDRiD} for nnU-Net on the same metric), and even when each automatic mask was given the class it overlaps most in the ground truth, an upper bound that reads the test labels, the \mAUPR{} reached only \rn{fm.zero.ub.value} (\rn{fm.zero.ub.model}). Text prompts were sensitive to wording: across three phrasings per lesion the best and worst AUPR differed \rn{fm.wording.ratio}-fold on average (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}c). With a box around every ground-truth component SAM~2 reached \rn{fm.oracle.all}, but an IDRiD image carries on average 44 microaneurysm and 142 hard exudate components; with 5 and 20 boxes per image and class the \mAUPR{} was \rn{fm.oracle.five} and \rn{fm.oracle.twenty} (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}b). Given the same boxes, the supervised models drew the lesions at least as well inside the box, so the gap of the promptable models lies in finding lesions, not in delineating them.

\end{simtext}
Among the trained settings, SAMed was the best parameter-efficient adaptation (0.460 $\pm$ 0.027 on DDR over three seeds, against 0.431 for nnU-Net), and full fine-tuning of MedSAM (0.413) was below every parameter-efficient adaptation of SAM except SAM2-UNet (Table~\ref{tab:foundation}). The pretraining of an encoder was then tested under one UPerNet head, one recipe and one training amount on DDR, in two pairs fixed in advance: an encoder pretrained on retinal images against its own starting point, and the same contrast under a second pretraining objective, so that the effect of retinal pretraining does not depend on one objective (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}d,e). In the first pair, RETFound's masked-autoencoder encoder (\rn{fm.enc.rm.int} internal, \rn{fm.enc.rm.TJDR-std} on the standard-field TJDR set) was below the ImageNet masked autoencoder it was initialised from (\rn{fm.enc.mi.int} and \rn{fm.enc.mi.TJDR-std}), by \rn{fm.enc.c1.int.abs} internally and \rn{fm.enc.c1.std.abs} on the standard-field TJDR set, with the only gain on the ultra-widefield images (\rn{fm.enc.rm.TJDR-uwf} against \rn{fm.enc.mi.TJDR-uwf}); on one seed, the retinal pretraining did not add to its starting point, and the ImageNet encoder under the same head exceeded every model of Table~\ref{tab:main} on DDR. The second pair, RETFound's DINOv2 encoder against DINOv2 itself, is pending.

\subsection{Effects of annotation granularity and training source}
The sources differ in how lesions were drawn before they differ in anything a model does (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}a). In the fine-grained sources an image carries many small components; in the coarse and region-consensus sources it carries few and large ones, which for the same retina means that several lesions and the tissue between them were enclosed by one outline.

To separate the effect of the training source from that of the model, the five representative models (U-Net, nnU-Net, M2MRF, SAM2-UNet and RETFound with UPerNet) were also trained on the standard-field TJDR images and on FGADR, at the pixel budget of DDR and with one seed, which together with the main table gives a matrix of four training sources by eight test sets (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}b,d; RETFound trained on TJDR is still running, so the TJDR-trained row is the mean of the other four models). Every source served its own test set best: in-source training gave 0.670 \mAUPR{} on IDRiD, 0.403 on DDR, 0.537 on the standard-field TJDR set and 0.411 on FGADR, against 0.368--0.584, 0.247--0.301, 0.450--0.523 and 0.159--0.232 from the other three sources (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}c). Training elsewhere cost 0.15 \mAUPR{} on average over the twelve off-diagonal cells and up to 0.30 (TJDR-trained models on IDRiD). Which foreign source was best depended on the labels of the test set rather than on the number of training images: DDR, the largest fine-grained source (383 images), was the best foreign source on every pixel-level test set (0.584 on IDRiD, 0.523 on the standard-field TJDR set, 0.391 on MAPLES-DR), whereas FGADR, three times larger (1,096 images) but drawn as mixed regions, was the best foreign source only on the two sets with coarse or region labels (0.232 on Retinal-Lesions and 0.270 on DiaRetDB1, against 0.201 and 0.259 from DDR) and lost 0.14 on average on the three fine-grained sources relative to in-source training; the standard-field TJDR set, the smallest source (172 images), transferred worst (0.368 on IDRiD, 0.247 on DDR).

We then added mixed or coarse labels to fine-label training while keeping the number of patch pixels fixed, for U-Net and M2MRF with one seed; differences smaller than twice the median seed standard deviation of the main table (0.018 on DDR) are reported without interpretation (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}e and Table~\ref{tab:factors}). On the DDR test set, adding the 1,096 FGADR images to DDR changed \mAUPR{} by $-$0.01 for U-Net (0.378 to 0.373) and $-$0.06 for M2MRF (0.380 to 0.320); adding only 383 FGADR images, as many as DDR has, by $-$0.01 and $-$0.04 (0.369 and 0.336), so the loss is not a matter of the fine images being outnumbered; adding Retinal-Lesions by $-$0.04 and $-$0.09 (0.341 and 0.286). On the IDRiD test set the same three training sets moved U-Net by +0.05, +0.03 and $-$0.03 (from 0.547) and M2MRF by $-$0.02, $-$0.02 and $-$0.06 (from 0.571), and on the standard-field TJDR and MAPLES-DR sets U-Net by $-$0.05 to +0.03 and M2MRF by $-$0.18 to $-$0.01. Mixed labels added to fine ones were therefore at best neutral and at worst costly, and coarse labels cost M2MRF 0.06--0.18 on every fine-grained test set. Trained on Retinal-Lesions alone, the two models scored 0.504 and 0.469 on its own test split but 0.164 and 0.104 on DDR and 0.248 and 0.167 on IDRiD; trained on FGADR alone, 0.364 and 0.437 on IDRiD.

A revision of the labels themselves, as opposed to a change of source, moved the scores far less. A refined annotation of the 27 IDRiD test images was released in 2026 (Refined IDRiD; one class per pixel, 1,024-pixel crops of the retinal region). We located each crop in the original image (every crop is the retinal bounding box padded to a square and resized; normalised cross-correlation with the original $\geq$ 0.9988 for all 27), resampled the official labels through the same geometry, and scored the test maps of the \rn{ref.n} IDRiD-trained models of Table~\ref{tab:main} against both label sets without retraining or re-inference. The revision changed 13 of the 27 images for microaneurysms and hemorrhages, 14 for hard exudates and 6 of 14 for soft exudates, moving 5.7\%, 1.6\%, 4.0\% and 0.7\% of the labelled pixels (pooled intersection over union 0.94, 0.98, 0.96 and 0.99) and no component count except hard exudates (3,895 to 3,882). Under the same protocol the \mAUPR{} of every model changed by between \rn{ref.delta.min} and \rn{ref.delta.max} (microaneurysm AUPR by \rn{ref.MA.delta.mean} on average, the other classes by less than 0.002), and the order of the \rn{ref.n} models was unchanged (Kendall $\tau$ = \rn{ref.tau}). The 1,024-pixel re-scoring itself stayed within \rn{ref.geometry.absmax} \mAUPR{} of the main-table test at the 1,440-pixel canvas ($\tau$ = \rn{ref.geometry.tau}). What separates the sources in Fig.~\ref{fig:sources} is therefore how lesions were defined and outlined, which a correction of individual masks within one style does not touch (Supplementary Table~8).

\subsection{Effects of lesion size, input resolution and training amount}
Lesion size is the property that separates the four classes most clearly (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}a). On the training images the median diameter of a microaneurysm is 0.5\% of the field-of-view diameter and that of a hemorrhage 1.1\%; we froze these two values, computed on training images only, as the boundaries between small, medium and large lesions before the main-table training. Resizing a whole image to a small input removes lesions from the training target itself: at 224 pixels \rn{vanish.IDRiD.224.MA}\% of the microaneurysms and \rn{vanish.IDRiD.224.EX}\% of the hard exudates of the IDRiD training set no longer cover a single pixel, and on DDR \rn{vanish.DDR.224.MA}\% and \rn{vanish.DDR.224.EX}\%; at 384 pixels the DDR figures are \rn{vanish.DDR.384.MA}\% and \rn{vanish.DDR.384.EX}\%, and on the 1,440-pixel canvas of the unified protocol essentially none are lost (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}b).

Lesion-level recall followed size for every model (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}c). On the DDR test set the median recall over the \rn{n.ranked} models (seed 0, threshold chosen on the validation set) was 0.26 for small, 0.49 for medium and 0.65 for large lesions; the best models detected 0.30 of the small ones (Swin-Unet and SAM2-UNet, nnU-Net ResEnc-M 0.29) and DeepLabv3, decoding at an output stride of 8, 0.07. By class, the median recall of small lesions was 0.46 for microaneurysms, 0.35 for hard exudates and 0.15 for hemorrhages, and the median lesion-level precision 0.30, 0.73 and 0.65. Input resolution acted mainly through this channel. Shrinking the canvas from 1,440 to 512 pixels at an equal pixel budget lowered \mAUPR{} on DDR from 0.380 to 0.358 for M2MRF and from 0.466 to 0.400 for RETFound with UPerNet (one seed), the recall of small lesions from 0.27 to 0.18 and from 0.29 to 0.12, and the microaneurysm AUPR from 0.148 to 0.114 and from 0.189 to 0.092 (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}d). Patches at native resolution, where a 960-pixel patch covers a smaller part of the retina and the fixed pixel budget buys fewer passes over each image, raised the small-lesion recall of M2MRF to 0.33 and of RETFound to 0.31, but lowered \mAUPR{} to 0.312 and 0.449: for RETFound the microaneurysm AUPR rose (0.189 to 0.196) while the hemorrhage and soft-exudate AUPR fell (0.491 to 0.460 and 0.546 to 0.513), so the smallest class gained from native resolution and the two largest lost.

More training images helped, with diminishing returns within the size of DDR. With 10\%, 50\% and 100\% of the DDR training set (38, 192 and 383 images; equal number of patch pixels, so each image is seen ten, two and one times as often), \mAUPR{} on the DDR test set was 0.321, 0.373 and 0.378 for U-Net and 0.307, 0.342 and 0.380 for M2MRF, and on IDRiD 0.492, 0.540 and 0.547 and 0.473, 0.549 and 0.571 (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}e). Merging the three fine-grained sources IDRiD, DDR and standard-field TJDR (599 images) at the same budget gave 0.367 and 0.368 on DDR, within 0.01 of DDR alone, and raised the scores on the sets outside the training sources by 0.01--0.04 (MAPLES-DR 0.372 and 0.349 against 0.329 and 0.324; FGADR 0.192 and 0.224 against 0.170 and 0.212; Retinal-Lesions 0.177 and 0.153 against 0.142 and 0.150), the gain of more fine-grained images rather than of in-source data. A source merged into the training set raised the score on its own test set by more, 0.05--0.07 (standard-field TJDR 0.562 and 0.565 against 0.494 and 0.506; IDRiD 0.607 and 0.619 against 0.547 and 0.571), which is the in-source gain of the matrix above and not generalization; these two tests are in-source for the merged run and are marked as such in Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}e. Exchanging the loss of U-Net with the architecture fixed moved \mAUPR{} on DDR between 0.357 (cross-entropy alone) and 0.378 (cross-entropy with batch Dice, the main table); Dice alone gave 0.237, the per-image Dice of the registered recipe 0.119, the collapse described above (Extended Data Fig.~8).

\subsection{Computational cost}
All models were trained on single 40\,GB cards, one task per card. Because the training amount is fixed in pixels, the cost of a model under the unified protocol reflects its throughput and its patch size: one seed on IDRiD took from \rn{cost.min} GPU hours (HRDecoder and U-Net) to \rn{cost.max} (\rn{cost.max.model}), and a DDR seed \rn{cost.ddr.ratio} times as long (median ratio), the slowest being the models that process small patches with sliding windows (FCT, \rn{cost.ddr.max} hours on DDR; SAM-Adapter 26, HACDR-Net 28 and CLC-Net 39 hours). nnU-Net follows its own schedule of 1,000 epochs rather than the pixel budget (7 hours per source, 11--13 for the residual-encoder plan). The two models that led the tables were not the costliest: SAMed took 2.1 and 7.4 hours and RETFound with UPerNet 6.1 and 24 hours per seed. Accuracy was not related to cost or to parameter count (Extended Data Fig.~10 and Supplementary Table~7).
