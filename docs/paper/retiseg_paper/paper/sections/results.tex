\section{Results}

% ============================================================== R1
\subsection{Study design}
We separated three properties that a published score combines: whether the number can be reproduced, how the model performs when training and evaluation are fixed, and whether its performance carries over to data from other sources (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}a). Eight test sets were prepared from seven datasets, \rn{n.images} images in total (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}b and Table~\ref{tab:datasets}). IDRiD and DDR are the training sources, because both have official splits and almost every published number refers to one of them. No model in the main tables was trained on the other six test sets, which we call independent. The sources also differ in how lesions were drawn. Following Playout and Cheriet\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization}, we assigned each source to one of four annotation granularity tiers: fine, mixed, coarse and region consensus.

From a ledger of 86 records (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}d; Methods) we registered \rn{n.models} model variants in five families. Each has a reproducibility level from R0 (no code) to R4 (training code, weights, splits and environment) (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}c and Table~\ref{tab:models}). Tier~1 holds the \rn{n.tier1} variants of the main comparisons: general segmentation networks (U-Net\cite{ronneberger2015unet}, DeepLabv3\cite{chen2017deeplabv3,chen2018deeplabv3plus}, HRNet\cite{wang2020hrnet}, Swin-Unet\cite{cao2022swinunet}, FCT\cite{tragakis2023fct} and H2Former\cite{he2023h2former}), the self-configuring nnU-Net\cite{isensee2021nnunet}, seven fundus-specific networks (M2MRF\cite{liu2023m2mrf}, HACDR-Net\cite{xu2024hacdrnet}, HRDecoder\cite{ding2024hrdecoder}, PMCNet\cite{he2022pmcnet}, WSRFNet\cite{li2024wsrfnet}, CLC-Net\cite{wang2023clcnet} and TC-Net\cite{zhang2023tcnet}) and eight foundation models (SAM\cite{kirillov2023sam}, SAM~2\cite{ravi2024sam2}, SAM~3\cite{carion2025sam3}, MedSAM\cite{ma2024medsam}, SAMed\cite{zhang2023samed}, SAM-Adapter\cite{chen2023samadapter}, SAM2-UNet\cite{xiong2024sam2unet} and RETFound\cite{zhou2023retfound}). H2Former exceeds the memory of one card at the unified patch size and is deferred. Tier~2 (\rn{n.tier2} variants, such as L-Seg\cite{guo2019lseg} and RTNet\cite{huang2022rtnet}) is registered for later rounds, and tier~3 (\rn{n.tier3} variants, including semi-supervised and text-prompted methods\cite{zhou2019collaborativesemisupervised,ullah2023ssmdunet,li2024tpdrseg}) is audited from the papers only.

Each property is examined with its own evaluations, and their results are never mixed in one table column. Numerical reproducibility is examined with the paper audit, the released checkpoints (author weights) and reruns of the authors' pipelines (author recipes). Performance under controlled conditions is examined by retraining the tier-1 models under one protocol, the unified protocol, and testing them on the official test set of their training source (internal test). Generalization is examined by applying every checkpoint unchanged to the independent test sets, and by varying the training source, annotation granularity, input resolution and training amount one at a time (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}e). The primary metric is the exact AUPR of each lesion class over all field-of-view pixels of a test set, averaged over the four classes (\mAUPR).

\subsection{Published numbers are not comparable across papers}
Few methods can be rerun at all. Of the \rn{funnel.dr} fundus-specific methods in the registry, \rn{funnel.code} have author code, \rn{funnel.train} have training code, and \rn{funnel.full} come with weights, splits and an environment description (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}a).

Papers state their split but not their metric. We extracted the setup and comparison tables of \rn{lit.n.methods} method papers, \rn{lit.base.n} baseline AUPR values on IDRiD and DDR in total (Supplementary Tables~1 and 2). Most papers use the official split: \rn{lit.idrid.official} of the \rn{lit.idrid.n} papers on IDRiD and \rn{lit.ddr.official} of the \rn{lit.ddr.n} on DDR. Of the \rn{lit.impl.n} papers whose main metric is AUPR, \rn{lit.impl.notstated} do not say how it was computed, and \rn{lit.impl.fixed} integrate over a fixed grid of thresholds, the IDRiD challenge protocol carried on by the evaluation code released with L-Seg and M2MRF. No paper states whether pixels outside the field of view were excluded (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}b).

Baseline numbers are mostly carried from earlier papers. Only \rn{lit.base.rerun}\% of the baseline values were produced by the paper that reports them. Another \rn{lit.base.copied1}\% repeat the value in the method's own paper and \rn{lit.base.copied2}\% repeat a value that an earlier paper had already carried; \rn{lit.base.unverified}\% cite a source outside the ledger or none. When a method is rerun, the value changes: L-Seg is reported on IDRiD with \mAUPR{} from \rn{lit.lseg.lo} to \rn{lit.lseg.hi} across \rn{lit.lseg.n} papers, and U-Net from \rn{lit.unet.lo} to \rn{lit.unet.hi} across \rn{lit.unet.n} (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}c).

\subsection{The evaluation implementation changes scores and ranks}
The widely reused 11-threshold implementation inflates AUPR. Its lowest threshold is 0, at which every pixel with a non-zero probability counts as positive. This adds a point with a recall of 1 and a precision equal to the prevalence, which enlarges the area under the curve. For the released M2MRF checkpoint, the authors' function returns \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.fn} on IDRiD and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.fn} on DDR. The exact AUPR inside the field of view is \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.exact} and \rn{e1.m2mrf.DDR.exact} (Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}d and Table~\ref{tab:original}). The authors' function reproduced their repository values to the last printed digit, and the field of view and overlapping labels changed only the third decimal (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}a,b). The gap between paper and protocol is therefore a property of the metric.

The inflation differs between models, so it changes their order. Applied to the maps of the converged retrained models, the 11-threshold implementation changed \mAUPR{} on IDRiD by $-$0.010 to +0.023. On DDR, where the low-probability tail of the maps carries more weight, it added 0.004 to 0.084 (Supplementary Table~5). The largest additions went to the least calibrated maps, and \rn{impl.moved} of the \rn{n.main} models changed rank (Kendall $\tau$ = \rn{impl.tau}; Fig.~\ref{fig:literature}e).

Released training code did not reproduce the released weights. We reran the M2MRF pipeline on IDRiD with the authors' batch size and schedule, three times. With the authors' metric, the three runs reached \rn{e1r.M2MRF.IDRiD.fn.lo}--\rn{e1r.M2MRF.IDRiD.fn.hi}, against \rn{e1.m2mrf.IDRiD.fn} for the released checkpoint (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}c). Every run was 5--7 points lower, about five times the spread between runs. HRDecoder, rerun once with its authors' recipe, reached \rn{e1r.HRDecoder.IDRiD.exact} under our protocol, 0.03 below its released checkpoint (0.707).

\subsection{Under controlled conditions the leading models cannot be separated}
For a controlled comparison we fixed everything that is not part of a method: data and splits, a canvas of 1,440 pixels across the field of view, the training amount counted in pixels, augmentation, checkpoint selection by validation \mAUPR, inference geometry and evaluation (Methods). Each model keeps its own loss, optimizer, learning rate, weight decay, schedule shape and effective batch (Table~\ref{tab:recipes}). Source recipes could not serve as common ground, because moved to IDRiD unchanged they differ about 67-fold in the number of training images seen. We retrained \rn{n.main} models on IDRiD and on DDR, nine of them with three seeds (Supplementary Table~3). Tables and tests use the mean over seeds.

Two adapted foundation models and one general network led both internal tests: SAMed, RETFound with a UPerNet head\cite{xiao2018upernet} and HRNet-W48 (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}a and Table~\ref{tab:main}). SAMed is a low-rank adaptation\cite{hu2022lora} of the SAM ViT-B\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} encoder. On IDRiD, SAM-Adapter scored level with SAMed. The differences among the leading models were within their variation between seeds. On DDR, SAMed and RETFound were 0.013 and 0.004 above HRNet-W48, and their own seed standard deviations were 0.027 and 0.018. Their 95\% bootstrap intervals over test images overlap (Supplementary Table~4). The table as a whole spans 0.41 \mAUPR{} on IDRiD and 0.28 on DDR. IDRiD has only 27 test images, so we base comparisons between architectures on DDR and on the independent tests.

Lesion-specific designs did not lead. The best fundus-specific network on DDR, CLC-Net, was 0.05 below SAMed. M2MRF and HRDecoder add lesion-specific decoders to the HRNet-W48 backbone, and both scored below the general HRNet-W48 head on both sources. The released HRDecoder checkpoints score higher than its unified run; a case study traces this difference to parts of the authors' recipe that the unified protocol replaces (Supplementary Note~2).

Published rankings did not predict the controlled ranking. For the \rn{tau.published.n} models that report an IDRiD \mAUPR, the published order and the order after retraining were unrelated (Kendall $\tau$ = \rn{tau.published}, $P$ = \rn{tau.published.p}; Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}f).

The largest departures came from recipe components. DeepLabv3 decodes at an output stride of 8 and lost the smallest class (microaneurysm AUPR 0.043 on DDR). FCT, trained from scratch at a constant learning rate, did not converge within the budget. A Dice term computed per image drove the rare-class channels to zero on DDR, where 83\% of the training patches contain no soft exudate. This affected the registered recipes of U-Net, SAM2-UNet and MedSAM. Pooling the Dice term over the batch raised their DDR \mAUPR{} from 0.119, 0.210 and 0.265 to 0.378, 0.360 and 0.413 (seed 0). For SAM2-UNet and MedSAM this revision followed a rule registered before training; for U-Net it was made after the registered run had been scored (Methods). Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the revised runs, and Supplementary Table~6 the registered ones. Other choices mattered less. With cross-entropy alone instead of cross-entropy and a pooled Dice term, U-Net lost 0.02 on DDR (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{edfig:loss}). A threefold change of the learning rate reordered HRNet-W48 and M2MRF on validation only at the lower rate (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{edfig:lr}).

\subsection{Foundation models depend on how they are used}
Results for segmentation foundation models are often reported without stating how the model was used. We separated four uses and do not rank them against each other (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}a and Table~\ref{tab:foundation}): zero-shot use, prompts derived from the ground truth, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and an encoder with a trained decoder or full fine-tuning.

Without training, the models did not find lesions. The automatic masks of SAM and SAM~2 reached an any-lesion AUPR of at most \rn{fm.any.samtwo.IDRiD} on IDRiD, against \rn{fm.any.ref.nnunet.IDRiD} for nnU-Net. Text prompts to SAM~3 found hard exudates and hemorrhages but not microaneurysms, and the wording of the prompt changed the result as much as the lesion class (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}c). Given a box around every lesion, by contrast, SAM delineated lesions as well as the supervised models within the same boxes (\rn{fm.box.sam.IDRiD} against \rn{fm.boxref.nnunet.IDRiD} for nnU-Net on IDRiD). The deficit of the promptable models lies in finding lesions, not in drawing them (Supplementary Note~2).

Once trained on the task, the adaptations competed with the best supervised networks, as described above. Among them, the parameter-efficient SAMed was the strongest, and full fine-tuning of MedSAM did not exceed it.

Retinal pretraining did not improve the encoder. We compared two retinal encoders with their own starting points under one UPerNet head, one recipe and one training amount (Fig.~\ref{fig:foundation}d; Supplementary Table~10). On DDR, RETFound's masked-autoencoder encoder scored \rn{fm.enc.c1.int.abs} below the ImageNet masked autoencoder\cite{he2022mae} it was initialised from. RETFound's DINOv2 encoder scored \rn{fm.enc.c2.int.abs} below DINOv2\cite{oquab2024dinov2}. Both differences had the same sign on IDRiD. The two natural-image encoders, run as single-seed controls, exceeded every model of Table~\ref{tab:main} on both sources.

\subsection{Across sources, rankings hold only where annotations are drawn alike}
A new source did not by itself cost performance. The DDR-trained models scored higher on IDRiD and on the standard-field TJDR set than on their own test set, and the same on MAPLES-DR (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}b,c and Table~\ref{tab:independent}). These three sets share fine-grained pixel labels and a standard field of view.

Different annotation conventions and imaging fields did cost performance. The same models lost a third to a half of their internal score on the mixed and coarse labels of FGADR and Retinal-Lesions, on the region consensus of DiaRetDB1 and on the 133$^\circ$ ultra-widefield TJDR images. Every model was lower on each of these four sets. The models trained on the 44 IDRiD images scored higher internally but lower on every other source, including the fine-grained ones. On the six independent sets, the DDR-trained checkpoint of a model was better than its IDRiD-trained checkpoint in \rn{indep.pairs.ddrbetter} of \rn{indep.pairs.n} pairs. The released author checkpoints showed the same pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:original}d).

Internal rank predicted independent rank to the extent that labels and imaging matched. For DDR-trained models, the Spearman correlation between the internal score and the score on the three fine-grained sets was 0.82--0.92. On the mixed, coarse and region-labelled sets it was 0.76--0.79, and DeepLabv3, tenth internally, ranked first on Retinal-Lesions and DiaRetDB1, because its output stride of 8 yields region-sized predictions. On the ultra-widefield images the correlation vanished ($\rho$ = \rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-uwf.rho}). Averaged by family, the adapted foundation models were ahead both internally and on the fine-grained independent sets, and the fundus-specific and general networks were level (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}d).

Over all eight test sets, \rn{cd.ntop} of the \rn{n.ranked} models could not be separated from the first. The Friedman test rejected equal ranks ($\chi^2$ = \rn{cd.chi2}, $P$ \rn{cd.p}), and \rn{cd.first.model} had the best average rank, but the Nemenyi critical difference with eight blocks is \rn{cd.value} ranks (Fig.~\ref{fig:retrained}e). RETFound's pretraining corpus contains the EyePACS images from which Retinal-Lesions and part of FGADR are drawn (Methods); without these two sets its rank is unchanged (fourth).

Absolute scores also depend on the disease stage of the test images. On the DDR test set, the median \mAUPR{} of the DDR-trained models was \rn{clin.ddr.lo.maupr} on images of grades 0--1 and \rn{clin.ddr.hi.maupr} on images of grades 2--4 (Supplementary Note~2). Scores are therefore comparable within a test set, not across test sets.

\subsection{Annotation conventions decide which training data transfer}
The sources differ in how lesions were outlined (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}a; Methods). In IDRiD and DDR, lesions are drawn as many small components, with a median diameter of about 0.5\% of the field-of-view width. In Retinal-Lesions and DiaRetDB1, components are few and eight to nine times larger for hard exudates, so one outline encloses several lesions and the tissue between them. FGADR lies between the two.

The labels of the test set, not the size of the training set, decided which source transferred best. We trained five representative models on two further sources, the standard-field TJDR images and FGADR, at the pixel budget of DDR (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}b--d). Every source served its own test set best, and training elsewhere cost \rn{cross.mean} \mAUPR{} on average. DDR (383 images) was the best foreign source on every standard-field test set with pixel-level labels. FGADR is three times larger but labelled as mixed regions. It was the best foreign source only on the coarse and region-labelled sets and on the ultra-widefield images.

Coarse labels added to fine ones did not help. At a fixed number of patch pixels, we added Retinal-Lesions or FGADR images to DDR training for U-Net and M2MRF (Fig.~\ref{fig:sources}e and Table~\ref{tab:factors}). Adding Retinal-Lesions lowered M2MRF by \rn{e4.m2mrf.retles.cost.lo}--\rn{e4.m2mrf.retles.cost.hi} on every standard-field fine-grained test set. Adding FGADR lowered M2MRF by 0.01--0.06, and moved U-Net by $-$0.01 to +0.05.

Correcting individual masks within one annotation style changed little. A refined annotation of the IDRiD test images has been published\cite{chankhachon2026refinedidrid}. Scored against it, the \mAUPR{} of the \rn{ref.n} IDRiD-trained models changed by at most 0.002, and their order did not change (Supplementary Note~2 and Supplementary Table~9).

\subsection{Imaging conditions limit the detection of small lesions}
Lesion classes differed more than models. The median AUPR over the \rn{n.main} models was 0.87 for hard exudates and 0.44 for microaneurysms on IDRiD, and 0.60 and 0.15 on DDR (Supplementary Table~4). Lesion size is the main reason (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}a). Resizing a whole image to a small input removes lesions from the training target itself. At 224 pixels, \rn{vanish.DDR.224.MA}\% of the microaneurysms in the DDR training labels no longer cover a single pixel; on the 1,440-pixel canvas essentially none are lost (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}b).

Lesion-level recall followed size for every model (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}c). On the DDR test set, the median recall over models was 0.26 for small, 0.49 for medium and 0.66 for large lesions. Input resolution acted through the small lesions. Shrinking the canvas from 1,440 to 512 pixels lowered DDR \mAUPR{} by \rn{e7.drop.aupr.range} for M2MRF and RETFound with UPerNet, and their recall of small lesions by \rn{e7.drop.small.pct.range}\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}d). Patches at native resolution raised small-lesion recall but lowered \mAUPR; for RETFound, the two smaller classes gained and the two larger classes lost.

The field of view had a larger effect than the inference scale. On the ultra-widefield images, the DDR-trained models changed by \rn{indep.DDR.TJDR-uwf.pct}\% relative to their internal score on average. Resizing these images to the 1,440-pixel canvas, instead of predicting at native resolution, moved the median \mAUPR{} by only +0.02 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{edfig:uwf}b).

More fine-grained images helped, with modest gains outside the training sources. Both U-Net and M2MRF improved from 10\% to 100\% of the DDR training set at an equal pixel budget (Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}e). Merging the three fine-grained sources (599 images) changed the DDR score by less than 0.02 and raised the scores on sets outside the training sources by 0.00--0.04.

\subsection{Ensembles, a reference model and cost}
Averaging the maps of several retrained models gave the highest scores of the study. The gain over the best single model was 0.02--0.06 \mAUPR{} on the fine-grained test sets and $-$0.01 to 0.02 on the others (Table~\ref{tab:independent}, lower block). A reference model with two members, RetiSeg-Ref, combines RETFound with UPerNet and SAMed trained on the merged fine-grained sources. On the four standard-field independent sets it came within \rn{n8.vs.all.absmax} of the ensemble of all 18 DDR-trained models (Supplementary Table~11).

Accuracy was not related to training cost, inference cost or parameter count (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{edfig:cost}). HRNet-W48 matched the two leading foundation models at a twentieth of the inference time of RETFound with UPerNet. Further results on ensembles, cost and agreement with human experts are given in Supplementary Note~2.
