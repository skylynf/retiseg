\begin{abstract}
Methods for segmenting diabetic retinopathy lesions in fundus photographs are usually compared through published scores on a few benchmark datasets. We asked whether such rankings can guide the choice of a method. To answer this, we separated three properties that a published score combines: numerical reproducibility, performance under controlled conditions, and generalization across data sources. We audited the reported numbers of \rn{lit.n.methods} method papers, rescored and reran released models, retrained \rn{n.main} models under one protocol, and tested them on eight test sets from seven datasets. Reported numbers depended on the evaluation implementation. A widely reused fixed-threshold AUPR inflated scores by model-dependent amounts and changed the order of models, and fewer than a third of baseline values had been rerun by the papers that report them. Under controlled conditions, published rankings did not predict performance, and the leading models could not be separated from one another. Across sources, scores and rankings held only where annotation conventions and imaging conditions matched those of the training data. Comparisons of lesion segmentation methods should therefore report these three properties separately. We will release the code, the splits, the probability maps and a reference model as a benchmark, RetiSeg\footnotemark.
\end{abstract}
\footnotetext{The RetiSeg code repository is at \url{https://github.com/skylynf/retiseg} and is still being refined; the probability maps and the weights of the reference model will be released there.}

% -------------------------------------------------------------- Introduction
\noindent Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults; about 103 million adults had DR in 2020, and 160.5 million are projected to have it by 2045\cite{cheung2010diabeticretinopathy,teo2021globalprevalence}. Screening programmes therefore photograph the fundus of people with diabetes at regular intervals and grade the images from visible lesions, chiefly microaneurysms (MA), hemorrhages (HE), hard exudates (EX) and soft exudates or cotton wool spots (SE)\footnote{The lesion terminology of this version follows the public datasets. We are reviewing these terms with clinicians and plan to revise them in a later version.}. The number, type and location of these lesions define the severity scales and the decision to refer\cite{etdrs1991report10,wilkinson2003icdr}. Deep networks now detect referable DR at the image level with high sensitivity and specificity\cite{gulshan2016jama,ting2017jama}, and an autonomous system has been authorized for use in primary care after a prospective pivotal trial\cite{abramoff2018pivotal}. Such classifiers do not show why an image was referred, however. Their sensitivity also varies widely in real-world screening\cite{lee2021sevensystems}, and the reference standard against which they are judged depends on the graders who produce it\cite{krause2018gradervariability}.

Lesion segmentation addresses these limits directly. A model that outlines lesions shows a grader why an image received its grade, supports screening decisions that must be auditable, and provides quantitative lesion burden for follow-up\cite{sebastian2023drlesionsurvey,tahir2024microaneurysmreview,dai2021deepdr}. Public datasets with pixel-level labels, first IDRiD\cite{porwal2018idrid,porwal2020idridchallenge} and DDR\cite{li2019ddr} and later FGADR\cite{zhou2021fgadr}, Retinal-Lesions\cite{wei2020retinallesionslesionnet}, TJDR\cite{mao2023tjdr} and MAPLES-DR\cite{maplesdr2024scientificdata}, have made the task a standard benchmark. New methods, from task-specific networks to adapted foundation models, are routinely ranked by their scores on IDRiD and DDR. Whether these rankings can guide the choice of a method depends on three properties that published comparisons rarely separate: whether a reported number can be reproduced, whether a difference persists when training and evaluation are fixed, and whether it carries over to data from other sources.

Each of these properties depends on factors outside the model. (1) The evaluation implementation. Papers differ in the split, the resolution at which predictions are scored, whether pixels outside the field of view count, and how the area under the precision--recall curve (AUPR) is computed. Such choices are known to reorder methods\cite{reinke2024pitfalls,maierhein2018rankings}. Baseline numbers are also carried between papers without being rerun, and overlap between training, pretraining and test images, a common source of optimistic results\cite{kapoor2023leakage}, has to be checked for each source. (2) The annotation conventions. Sources were labelled with polygons, ellipses or regions, by many graders or by a few experts\cite{kauppi2007diaretdb1}, so ``ground truth'' differs from one source to the next. A test split drawn from the training source therefore says little about performance elsewhere\cite{zech2018generalization}. (3) The imaging conditions. The lesions are small: shrinking an image to the 224-pixel input of a common backbone removes \rn{vanish.DDR.224.MA}\% of the microaneurysms from the DDR training labels. Fields of view also range from standard to ultra-widefield.

Earlier syntheses do not resolve these issues. Surveys collect the numbers that papers report\cite{sebastian2023drlesionsurvey,tahir2024microaneurysmreview}. The closest study\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization} showed with U-Net-type baselines that annotation style governs transfer between datasets, but it did not rerun fundus-specific methods, audit published numbers or include foundation models. Outside ophthalmology, standardized reassessments have found that part of the reported progress disappears once baselines are configured with care\cite{isensee2021nnunet,isensee2024revisited}. A recent assessment of T cell receptor--epitope predictors\cite{lu2026tcrepitopenaturemethods} supplies the design we follow: evaluate the original models, retrain everything that can be retrained under one protocol, and judge both on independent data.

We assessed DR lesion segmentation methods at each of the three levels. For numerical reproducibility, we audited the published numbers and their provenance, scored the released checkpoints and reran the authors' pipelines. For performance under controlled conditions, we retrained \rn{n.main} models under one protocol on two sources. For generalization, we tested every model on six test sets from five sources never used for training, and varied the training source, annotation granularity, input resolution and training amount one at a time. The findings are distilled into a reference model, RetiSeg-Ref. Because pixel-level labels are themselves graders' judgements, we are also reviewing model errors with ophthalmologists; this clinical review will be reported in a later version.
