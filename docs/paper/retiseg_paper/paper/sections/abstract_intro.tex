{\sffamily\bfseries\small\color{accent}Abstract\par}
\vspace{3pt}
{\sffamily\small
Pixel-level segmentation of diabetic retinopathy lesions in fundus photographs supports interpretable automated grading, and many methods report results on a few public datasets whose splits, input resolutions and metric implementations differ. Here we assessed \rn{n.models} model variants on \rn{n.sources} test sets from seven datasets (\rn{n.images} images) under one protocol. We audited the published numbers and their provenance, scored released checkpoints with the authors' metrics and with ours, retrained \rn{n.main} models with complete training code under a unified protocol, and tested every model on sources that were never used for training. Published values were often not comparable: the implementation of the area under the precision--recall curve differed between papers, and many baseline values had been carried over from earlier work. Under equal conditions, adapted foundation models and a general-purpose high-resolution network led the internal tests, and the differences among the leading models were within the seed-to-seed variation. On independent data, performance followed the annotation style and field of view of the test images more closely than the architecture, and the training source, the annotation granularity and the input resolution moved scores as much as the choice of model. We release the code, the splits, the probability maps and a reference model as a benchmark for this task\footnotemark.\par}
\end{tcolorbox}

% -------------------------------------------------------------- Introduction
\noindent Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is graded from lesions that are visible in colour fundus photographs: microaneurysms (MA), hemorrhages (HE), hard exudates (EX) and soft exudates or cotton wool spots (SE)\footnote{The lesion terminology of this version follows the public datasets. We are reviewing these terms with clinicians (for example, cotton wool spots in place of soft exudates, and the distinctions among hemorrhage and exudate types) and plan to revise them in a later version.}. A model that outlines these lesions pixel by pixel shows a grader why an image received its grade, supports screening programmes in which the decision must be auditable, and provides quantitative lesion burden for follow-up\cite{sebastian2023drlesionsurvey,tahir2024microaneurysmreview}. Public datasets with pixel-level labels, first IDRiD\cite{porwal2018idrid,porwal2020idridchallenge} and DDR\cite{li2019ddr} and later FGADR\cite{zhou2021fgadr}, Retinal-Lesions\cite{wei2020retinallesionslesionnet} and TJDR\cite{mao2023tjdr}, have turned the task into a standard benchmark, and methods built for it now span multi-scale convolutional networks\cite{guo2019lseg,liu2023m2mrf,xu2024hacdrnet,he2022pmcnet}, relation and transformer designs\cite{huang2022rtnet,he2023h2former}, semi-supervised schemes\cite{zhou2019collaborativesemisupervised,ullah2023ssmdunet} and adaptations of segmentation foundation models\cite{kirillov2023sam,ravi2024sam2,ma2024medsam,li2024tpdrseg}.

Several properties of the task make the reported progress hard to read. (1) The datasets are small and heterogeneous. IDRiD has 81 annotated images, its official test set 27, and soft exudates appear in about half of them; other sources were annotated with ellipses and polygons by many graders, or as regions with confidence levels by four experts\cite{kauppi2007diaretdb1}, so that ``ground truth'' means different things from one source to the next. (2) The lesions are small. A microaneurysm spans about half a percent of the field-of-view diameter, and shrinking an image to the 224-pixel input of a common backbone removes \rn{vanish.DDR.224.MA}\% of the microaneurysms and \rn{vanish.DDR.224.EX}\% of the hard exudates from the DDR training labels before any model is trained. (3) Evaluation is not standardized. Papers differ in the split (official or their own), the resolution at which predictions are scored, whether pixels outside the field of view count, and how the area under the precision--recall curve (AUPR) is computed; a widely reused implementation samples the curve at 11 thresholds. (4) Baseline numbers travel between papers without being rerun, sometimes across several generations and across different splits. (5) Claims about foundation models mostly come from the papers that propose an adaptation, with zero-shot use, prompting from ground truth and fine-tuning reported side by side.

Earlier syntheses do not resolve these issues. Surveys collect the numbers that papers report\cite{sebastian2023drlesionsurvey,tahir2024microaneurysmreview}. The closest study, by Playout and Cheriet\cite{playout2024crossdatasetgeneralization}, trained U-Net-type baselines across five datasets and showed that annotation style governs cross-dataset transfer; it did not rerun fundus-specific methods, did not audit published numbers and did not include foundation models. Outside ophthalmology, standardized reassessments have repeatedly found that part of the reported progress disappears once baselines are configured with care\cite{isensee2021nnunet,isensee2024revisited} and that rankings on small test sets are fragile\cite{maierhein2018rankings}. A recent assessment of T cell receptor--epitope predictors\cite{lu2026tcrepitopenaturemethods} supplies the design we follow: evaluate the original models, retrain everything that can be retrained under one protocol, and judge both on independent data.

We therefore assessed methods for DR lesion segmentation along five lines. (1) Systematic collection: a fixed literature search and a ledger of 86 records, \rn{n.sources} test sets with frozen splits, and \rn{n.models} registered model variants with a reproducibility level for each. (2) Audit of published numbers: which split, which metric implementation and which provenance stand behind each reported value. (3) Original models: released checkpoints scored with the authors' metric and with ours, and the authors' pipelines rerun from scratch. (4) Unified retraining: one protocol for data, training amount, augmentation, checkpoint selection and evaluation, up to three seeds, and six test sets from five sources that were never used for training. (5) Factors: annotation granularity, training source, training amount, input resolution, lesion size, loss and the way a foundation model is used, each varied alone. The study answers three questions: how many published numbers can be reproduced and where the gaps come from (Q1); which family of methods is better once conditions are equal, and whether the answer survives on independent data (Q2); and which properties of the data and the protocol decide performance (Q3). The findings are distilled into a reference model, RetiSeg-Ref, released with the benchmark as a starting point for new images.
